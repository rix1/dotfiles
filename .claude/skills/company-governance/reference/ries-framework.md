# Eric Ries, *Incorruptible* (2026): the vocabulary

Use these terms when writing the risk section, so the analysis maps onto the
book the user has read.

## Core ideas

- **Financial gravity**: the pull that success itself creates toward
  short-term extraction. Ownership, incentives, charters and accountability
  reshape behaviour as a company grows; failure is structural before it is
  ethical. Strongest at moments of success (a big round, an IPO, an
  acquisition offer), not at moments of failure.
- **Magnetic alignment**: the counter-force. A genuine mission plus
  trustworthiness pulls customers, talent and patient capital. Ries's
  examples: Cloudflare giving encryption away, Costco's fixed hot-dog price
  as "proof of character".
- **Mission primacy** replaces shareholder primacy. Ries argues the
  shareholder-primacy era is over on its own financial terms.
- **Structural integrity**: the protections that let a mission survive
  contact with financial gravity. "By the time you need it, it is too late to
  build it." Build before the money arrives.
- **Incorruptible blueprint**: (1) create something worth protecting, (2)
  build structural integrity around it.

## Structures he points to

- **Spiritual holding company / foundation ownership**: a non-profit
  foundation or trust holds control and safeguards purpose. Novo Nordisk,
  Grundfos, IKEA, Patagonia. His claim: such companies are several times
  more likely to reach year fifty.
- **Dual-entity model**: a for-profit board held accountable by an outside
  board of trustees.
- **Constitutional governance**: a written, binding statement of what the
  company exists to do, beyond a marketing tagline.
- **Super-voting or dual-class shares**: "the least bad of the current
  options", not a solution.
- **Long-Term Stock Exchange** and the critique of quarterly reporting;
  only about one in five founder-CEOs remain after a venture-backed IPO.
- **Public-benefit corporation** status as a minimal, cheap first step.

## Failure examples he uses

- FedMart / Sol Price: profitable and ethical, ousted by investors, then
  dismantled.
- Whole Foods: profitable every quarter, still acquired because its
  structure left it "defenceless".

## Gravity vectors to check in a private company

1. Preferred stock stack and liquidation preferences after several rounds.
2. A strategic corporate lead: information rights, board seat or observer,
   right of first refusal on a sale, conflicts with the company's customers.
3. Hedge-fund or late-stage money with a shorter horizon than the roadmap.
4. Thesis funds (climate, defense) whose thesis the narrative must keep
   satisfying.
5. Government grants and defense programmes: milestones, reporting, export
   control.
6. A revenue business subsidising a moonshot: every runway conversation
   becomes a resourcing conversation.
7. Secondary liquidity and employee tenders.
8. IPO and quarterly reporting.
9. Acquisition by a customer, partner or investor as the natural exit.
10. Founders diluted below control with no voting protection.

## The diagnostic question

Does the governance protect the mission *during success*, or only react
after corruption shows? And: were the protections in place before the first
institutional money?
